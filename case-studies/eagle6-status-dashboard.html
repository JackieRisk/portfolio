TL;DR

As Principal Product Designer at EAGLE6, I led two designers in building dashboards that gave the VA's migration teams a clear view into what their hospitals and clinics depended on, infrastructure-wise. The VA's systems had grown organically with no map, so any update had an unknown impact on hospitals, clinics, doctors, and patients. Our dashboards worked like a pane of glass: a technician could look at a hospital, or at a single piece of equipment, and ask, "if I turn this off, what is the impact?" We took the design through two rounds of testing and stakeholder feedback to a demo-ready dashboard, and delivered peace of mind to the migration teams.

The Challenge

The VA, a vast U.S. government organization, was running on extremely outdated technology, both physical and digital. Modernizing meant migrating from the legacy MUMPS system to Cerner. Over time, everything had grown organically, and there was no map of the infrastructure. No one could clearly see which systems supported, talked to, connected with, or depended on other systems.

That lack of clarity was the pain. Any update, like removing a single server, had an unknown impact on hospitals, clinics, doctors, and patients.

If we remove a server here, does it take down a hospital?

These were high-impact, high-risk decisions, on physical and digital systems alike. EAGLE6's goal was to enable the migration with little to no interruption to the doctors and veterans who depend on these systems to work and live.

Where EAGLE6 fit

EAGLE6 is an application built to secure large enterprises by detecting unknown vulnerabilities before they expose systems to risk. For the VA, that meant knowing which hospitals and clinics were not up to their gold standard and how to remediate that. EAGLE6 could also detect external links to non-networked systems and alert analysts, so that connections to things like heart monitors and EKG machines would not be interrupted.

My Role & Scope

I was the Principal Product Designer on this effort, leading two other designers. We designed the dashboards together, and I:

  • Set the design direction and led the two designers.
  • Hosted feedback sessions with the VA technicians handling the migration to understand their pain points and test our direction.
  • Worked with backend developers to determine what data we could surface.
  • Prototyped data visualization approaches and tested them with users across two rounds.
  • Built on my Section 508-compliant design system, and extended beyond it where it did not yet support data visualization.
  • Worked with development to get a demo-ready product in front of the business.

Leading the team

I hosted design critiques for my two designers. For this project I also took the Interaction Design Foundation course Information Visualization: Getting Dashboards Right ↗, and I encouraged my designers to take it with me.

Approach

1. Start with the people running the migration

Sit-down chats and feedback sessions with the VA technicians who handled the migration made the pain clear: a lack of clarity. They could not see which systems supported, talked to, connected with, or depended on other systems.

2. Ideate and prototype at the same time

Given the accelerated timeline, we started ideation and prototyping together to speed up solution development.

The working board, zoomed out.

3. Design a pane of glass around the data we could surface

The dashboards worked as a cross-section view that clarified what a hospital depends on, infrastructure-wise: servers, computers, and the rest. A technician could start from a hospital or from a specific piece of equipment and ask what the impact would be if it were turned off. Once I had the pain points, I worked with our developers to determine what data we could surface to answer that question.

4. Build on the design system, then go beyond it

All government work is legally required to be Section 508 compliant, so I built on my design system, which already met that bar. But the system did not have robust data visualization support, and dashboards required thinking outside of it. To close that gap, I took a dashboard design course and brought my designers along.

5. Test, then refine

Following multiple testing sessions and stakeholder feedback, we began a second iteration. With a better understanding of user needs, and the data our backend developers could make available, I prototyped several methods of data visualization to test with users. The timeline allowed two rounds of iterating, testing, and feedback, after which I worked with development to get a demo-ready product available to the business.

Results & Impact

  • Delivered peace of mind to the migration teams: a clear view into what a hospital or a single piece of equipment depended on before anyone changed it.
  • Delivered a functional, demo-ready dashboard within an accelerated timeline.
  • Completed two rounds of iteration, testing, and feedback before handing off to development.

Reflection

Dashboards pushed me past my own design system. It did not yet have robust data visualization support, so I had to think beyond it, and I learned a lot from the course I took alongside my designers. Data visualization is something I've become enamored with, and I continue to study how best to present data so users can consume it concisely and accurately, which matters most when the decision on the other end carries real risk.
