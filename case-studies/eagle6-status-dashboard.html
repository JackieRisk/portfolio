Designing dashboards that help VA stakeholders and analysts understand the impact of infrastructure changes during the move from MUMPS to Cerner.

TL;DR

As Principal Product Designer at EAGLE6, I designed dashboards for VA stakeholders and analysts during the department's migration from the legacy MUMPS system to the Cerner electronic health record platform. Before changing any piece of infrastructure, the VA needed to know what it touched. I worked with backend developers to decide which data to surface and how to visualize it, then took the design through two rounds of testing and stakeholder feedback to a demo-ready dashboard.

The Challenge

The VA, a vast U.S. government organization, was running on extremely outdated technology, both physical and digital. Modernizing meant migrating from the legacy MUMPS system to Cerner, and in a system that old, almost nothing can be changed in isolation. To update anything, the VA first needed to know the impact of any single piece of infrastructure.

If we remove a server here, does it take down a hospital?

These were high-impact, high-risk decisions. EAGLE6's goal was to enable the migration with little to no interruption to the doctors and veterans who depend on these systems to work and live.

Where EAGLE6 fit

EAGLE6 is an application built to secure large enterprises by detecting unknown vulnerabilities before they expose systems to risk. For the VA, that meant knowing which hospitals and clinics were not up to their gold standard and how to remediate that. EAGLE6 could also detect external links to non-networked systems and alert analysts, so that connections to things like heart monitors and EKG machines would not be interrupted.

A note on the imagery

Because of the security requirements, and the risk of exposing protected health information (PHI), I can't show anything real. Every image on this page is mockup imagery with scrubbed data, and the live app is not available to demo.

My Role & Scope

I was the Principal Product Designer on this effort and owned the end-to-end UX:

  • Turning the pain points of VA stakeholders and analysts into design problems.
  • Working with backend developers to determine what data we could surface to solve those problems.
  • Prototyping data visualization approaches, from whiteboard sketches to high-fidelity dashboards, and testing them with users.
  • Folding feedback from multiple testing sessions and stakeholders into a second iteration.
  • Working with development to get a demo-ready product in front of the business.

Approach

1. Ideate and prototype at the same time

Given the accelerated timeline, we started ideation and prototyping together to speed up solution development.

The working board, zoomed out.

2. Design around the data we could surface

Once I had the pain points, I worked with our developers to determine what data we could surface that would help solve the users' problems. The available data set the boundaries for what each dashboard could show.

3. Test, then refine

Following multiple testing sessions and stakeholder feedback, I began a second iteration. With a better understanding of user needs, and the data our backend developers could make available, I prototyped several methods of data visualization to test with users.

4. Hand off to a demo

The timeline allowed for two rounds of iterating, testing, and feedback. After that, I worked with development to get a demo-ready product available to the business.

Results & Impact

  • Delivered a functional, demo-ready dashboard for VA stakeholders and analysts within an accelerated timeline.
  • Completed two rounds of iteration, testing, and feedback before handing off to development.
  • Shaped what the dashboards could show by working directly with backend developers on the data that was available.

Reflection

The project ran on a tight timeline, and it worked because of close collaboration and agile design practices. It also deepened my interest in data visualization. I continue to study how best to present data so users can consume it concisely and accurately, which matters most when the decision on the other end carries real risk.
